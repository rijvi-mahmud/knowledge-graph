purpose: Add the chair, procedure and recall obligations that shape how a dental practice schedules its day.
scope:
  - Operatory (chair) as a bookable dimension alongside participants
  - Appointment length derived from the procedures' time patterns
  - Provider overlap across operatories, judged on provider time only
  - Completing attached procedures when the appointment completes
  - Recall due-date update when a recall trigger procedure completes
  - Recording broken appointments with their CDT codes
nonGoals:
  - Treatment plan creation, acceptance and financial estimates - owned by Treatment plans
  - Recall types, intervals and recall lists - owned by Recall
  - Charting, tooth conditions and clinical notes - owned by the clinical record

actors:
  - name: Provider
    description: The dentist the appointment is assigned to. Every dental appointment has one; a hygiene appointment also has a hygienist.
  - name: Hygienist
    description: The provider for the cleaning part of a hygiene appointment. Hygienist time counts as provider time.
  - name: Dental assistant
    description: Covers the non-provider time of a procedure - seating, setup, impressions, cleanup.
  - name: Treatment coordinator
    description: Books planned treatment from an accepted treatment plan and sequences multi-visit work.

glossary:
  - term: Operatory
    definition: A treatment room with a dental chair. The physical resource an appointment occupies.
  - term: CDT code
    definition: Code on Dental Procedures and Nomenclature, maintained by the ADA. Revised every year with changes effective 1 January; CDT 2026 added 31 codes and deleted 6.
  - term: Time pattern
    definition: The minute-by-minute shape of a procedure in the practice's time increment (10 minutes by default), each increment marked as provider time or non-provider (assistant) time.
  - term: Recall
    definition: A periodic preventive visit due at an interval set per patient. Prophy and perio maintenance are separate recall types; a patient is on one or the other.
  - term: Frequency limitation
    definition: A payer rule limiting how often a procedure is covered - for example twice per benefit year, twice per calendar year, or once every six months.
  - term: Broken appointment
    definition: A missed or late-cancelled appointment, recorded with CDT D9986 (missed) or D9987 (cancelled).
  - term: ASAP
    definition: A flag on a booked appointment meaning the patient wants an earlier slot if one opens.

concepts:
  - name: Column scheduling
    description: One dentist working several operatories in parallel, present in each chair only for their provider time.
  - name: Attached procedure
    description: A treatment-planned procedure linked to an appointment. Completing the appointment completes it; detaching it returns it to the treatment plan.
  - name: Recall trigger
    description: The procedures (for example a prophylaxis or periodic evaluation) whose completion restarts a recall type's interval.

businessRules:
  - id: BR-4
    text: A patient cannot be double-booked, and an operatory cannot be unless the practice allows overlap. A provider may overlap across operatories; overlapping provider time is shown as a conflict and blocked only for procedures the practice marks as not double-bookable.
    rationale: A dentist runs several chairs while assistants and hygienists work, so blocking every overlap would cut what the practice can treat. Some procedures need the dentist's full attention, which is why blocking is set per procedure rather than globally.
  - id: BR-D1
    text: Appointment length is the provider time of all attached procedures added together, plus the longest non-provider time of any single procedure. A time-locked appointment keeps its pattern when procedures change.
    rationale: Assistant setup and cleanup happen once per visit, not once per procedure, so adding them up overstates the visit. Time lock exists because staff often know a visit needs a length the catalog cannot predict.
  - id: BR-D2
    text: Completing an appointment completes every attached procedure, dated that day and credited to the appointment's providers. A procedure that was not performed must be detached first and returns to the treatment plan.
    rationale: Treatment changes chairside, and completed procedures go straight to the ledger and the claim. Detaching before completing is what keeps work that was not done from being billed.
  - id: BR-D3
    text: Completing a recall trigger procedure sets that recall type's next due date to the completion date plus the patient's interval. The next visit is never booked automatically.
    rationale: Auto-booked visits the patient never agreed to turn into no-shows and block chairs. Booking the next visit at checkout works well, but as a conversation, not a side effect.
  - id: BR-D4
    text: Booking a procedure before the payer's next covered date flags the appointment; the booking proceeds.
    rationale: Plans count frequency differently (per benefit year, per calendar year, per six months), and the patient may choose to pay. A surprise bill is the most common dental billing complaint, so the front desk has to see the flag at booking.
  - id: BR-D5
    text: A missed appointment is recorded with D9986 and a late cancellation with D9987. For Medicaid patients the fee is always zero.
    rationale: CMS prohibits charging Medicaid beneficiaries for missed or cancelled appointments, but a recorded history of broken appointments is still needed to manage attendance.

validations:
  - field: operatory_id
    rule: must not overlap an active appointment in the same operatory, unless the practice allows overlap
    error: APPOINTMENT_OPERATORY_TAKEN
  - field: time_pattern
    rule: provider time must not overlap the same provider's time in another active appointment, when either appointment has a procedure marked not double-bookable
    error: APPOINTMENT_PROVIDER_TIME_CONFLICT
  - field: procedures
    rule: at least one entry, each a CDT code valid on the appointment date
    error: APPOINTMENT_INVALID_PROCEDURES

workflows:
  - name: Book planned treatment
    trigger: A patient accepts a treatment plan
    actor: Treatment coordinator
    steps:
      - Group the planned procedures to schedule together
      - Derive the time pattern from the procedure catalog
      - Ask Calendar for slots where the operatory is free and the provider's time fits
      - Run the healthcare booking workflow to completion
      - Attach the procedures to the appointment
    outcome: The visit is booked with its procedures, length and chair fixed.
  - name: Hygiene appointment with exam
    trigger: A recall visit is booked
    actor: Front desk
    steps:
      - Book a hygiene operatory with the hygienist and the dentist as provider
      - Attach the cleaning and the periodic evaluation
      - Check the dentist's exam time against the dentist's other chairs
    outcome: One appointment carries both providers, each counted only for their own time.
  - name: Checkout
    trigger: The patient leaves the chair
    actor: Front desk
    steps:
      - Review the attached procedures against the provider's notes
      - Detach anything not performed, so it returns to the treatment plan
      - Attach anything performed but not planned
      - Set the appointment complete, which completes its procedures and emits appointment.procedures_completed
      - Offer to book the next visit if a recall came due
    outcome: The chart and the ledger receive what was actually done.
  - name: Fill a cancellation
    trigger: An appointment is cancelled
    actor: Front desk
    steps:
      - Find ASAP-flagged appointments whose length and provider fit the freed slot
      - Offer the slot to those patients
      - Reschedule the patient who accepts and clear their ASAP flag
    outcome: The chair is refilled instead of standing empty.

  - id: AC-D4

    decision: The operatory is its own booking dimension, not a participant.
    rationale: Chairs get no reminders, give no consent and have no identity. As participants they would inherit rules that do not apply to them.
    alternatives: Model the chair as a participant - rejected, it breaks the participant count and consent rules.
    date: '2026-10-04'
  - id: ADR-D2
    decision: Provider overlap is allowed by default and judged on provider time only; blocking is opt-in per procedure.
    rationale: This is how dental practice management systems behave, and it matches column scheduling. A global block would forbid the way most general practices run.
    alternatives: Block any provider-time overlap - rejected, makes a hygiene exam impossible while the dentist works another chair.
    date: '2026-10-04'

A dental practice schedules chairs as much as people. Every visit occupies an
operatory, its length comes from the procedures attached to it, and the dentist
is rarely in one room for the whole of it. The [healthcare
layer](/docs/healthcare/appointment) still applies in full: consent at check-in,
eligibility that never blocks care.

## The chair is booked, not just the dentist

An operatory is a room with a chair and equipment, and normally only one patient
fits in it. A booking that finds the dentist free but the chair taken cannot go
ahead, so the operatory is checked on every booking alongside the patient. Some
practices allow overlap in a chair, for example a quick post-op check while the
previous patient is numbing, so this is a setting, not a law.

The chair is deliberately not a participant. Participants are people who
consent, get reminders and can be contacted. A chair does none of those things.

## A dentist works several chairs at once

In a typical general practice the dentist runs two or three operatories in
parallel. An assistant seats and prepares the patient, the dentist does the part
only a dentist can do, and the assistant finishes while the dentist moves to the
next chair. A hygiene appointment works the same way: the hygienist cleans, and
the dentist steps in for the exam.

That is why the rule against overlapping appointments changes here. Only
provider time counts. Overlaps are allowed and shown as conflicts on the
schedule, so the front desk can see a dentist who is stretched. Procedures that
need the dentist's full attention can be marked so they are never double-booked.

## Procedures set the length of the visit

Each procedure in the catalog has a time pattern in the practice's time
increment, ten minutes by default. Each increment is either provider time or
assistant time.

When procedures are combined, provider time adds up, but assistant time does
not. Seating, setup and cleanup happen once per visit, so the visit takes the
longest assistant time of any one procedure. Adding them all would book every
multi-procedure visit too long. When staff know better than the catalog, they
lock the time pattern and later procedure changes leave it alone.

## What was planned is not what was done

The procedures attached to an appointment come from the treatment plan, and
treatment changes chairside. A filling becomes a crown when the tooth turns out
to be cracked, or a procedure is postponed because the patient is in pain.

Completing the appointment completes every attached procedure and sends it to
the ledger and the claim. So checkout comes first: anything not performed is
detached and goes back to the treatment plan, and anything done but not planned
is attached. Skipping that step bills for work that was not done.

## Recall is updated, not booked

Each recall type has trigger procedures. When one completes, the next due date
moves to the completion date plus the patient's interval. Many practices use six
months and a day for prophy, so a visit on the due date never falls before the
payer's covered date. Evidence supports setting the interval by risk: UK NICE
guidance puts it between 3 and 24 months for adults.

The next visit is not booked automatically. Visits booked without the patient
agreeing turn into no-shows. Booking before the patient leaves works well, but
it is a conversation at checkout.

## Broken appointments are coded, not just counted

A missed appointment is recorded as D9986 and a late cancellation as D9987, so
attendance history sits in the same record as treatment. Payers rarely
reimburse either, and for Medicaid patients the fee must be zero: CMS prohibits
charging them for broken appointments. The code is still recorded, because a
pattern of broken appointments is what a practice's attendance policy acts on.
