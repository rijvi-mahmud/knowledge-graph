---
title: Consent
description: 'What patients have agreed to, refused or restricted: consent to care, authorizations to share records, restrictions, opt-outs, privacy notices and advance directives, and whether a use or disclosure is allowed.'
module: consent
version: 0.4.3
status: draft
industry: healthcare
purpose: Record every decision a patient, or someone acting for them, makes about their care and their health information, prove it was made validly, and answer every module whether a use or disclosure of a patient's data is allowed, with the obligations that come with it. Works in the United States and the European Union.
scope:
- Consent to treatment and informed consent to a procedure, with who obtained it
- In the United States, HIPAA authorizations, 42 CFR Part 2 consents, restriction requests, requests not to share electronic health information, and the Notice of Privacy Practices acknowledgment
- In the European Union, GDPR consent where consent is the legal basis, privacy information under Articles 13 and 14, and EHDS restrictions and opt-outs
- Revocation, expiry and refusal of any of them
- Whether a patient has an advance directive, and its document
- Opt-outs from contact channels
- Answering whether a use or disclosure of a patient's data is allowed, and recording each answer
- Recording emergency use of restricted data
nonGoals:
- Research consent and ethics committee approval. A Research module owns them.
- What an advance directive says and the orders it leads to, such as a do-not-resuscitate order. Orders owns orders; this module records that a directive exists and holds its document.
- Who may act for a patient, and in what scope. The Patients module records it; this module asks.
- Releasing copies of the record and the accounting of disclosures. Release of information owns them, asking this module before each disclosure.
- Labelling data as sensitive. The module that owns the data sets its sensitivity codes; this module reads them.
- The content of the Notice of Privacy Practices and the organization's privacy policies. The organization writes them; this module records who received which version.
actors:
- name: Registrar
  description: Gives notices, records consents and authorizations at registration and admission.
- name: Clinician
  description: Obtains informed consent for treatment and procedures, and records emergency use of restricted data.
- name: Privacy officer
  description: Decides restriction requests and their termination, and reviews emergency uses.
- name: Patient
  description: A patient, or a representative within their authority, using the portal or signing on paper.
- name: System
  description: Other modules asking whether a use or disclosure is allowed.
assumptions:
- id: AS-H1
  text: Each module that owns data labels it with sensitivity codes, such as substance use disorder or reproductive health.
  rationale: This module decides from labels; it can't know what a note or result is about.
- id: AS-H2
  text: The Patients module records who may act for a patient, in what scope, and whether the patient is alive and a minor.
  rationale: Who may sign is decided from that record, not here.
- id: AS-H3
  text: Modules ask before each use or disclosure that could need consent and state its purpose.
  rationale: A consent nobody checks protects no one.
glossary:
- term: Consent
  definition: A recorded decision by a patient, or someone acting for them, to permit or deny something, such as a treatment or a disclosure (FHIR Consent).
- term: Authorization
  definition: In the United States, the signed permission HIPAA requires for a use or disclosure the law doesn't otherwise allow (45 CFR 164.508).
- term: Part 2 program
  definition: A federally assisted program that provides substance use disorder treatment, whose records 42 CFR Part 2 protects.
- term: Restriction
  definition: A patient's request to limit uses or disclosures for treatment, payment or operations (45 CFR 164.522), or, in the EU, to limit professionals' access (EHDS Article 8).
- term: Opt-out
  definition: A patient's choice not to take part, such as in the EU from access through access services (EHDS Article 10) or from secondary use (EHDS Article 71), or from a contact channel.
- term: Disclosure check
  definition: 'A question from a module: may this data be used or disclosed, for this purpose, to this recipient? The answer is permit or deny, with its basis and obligations.'
- term: Advance directive
  definition: A written instruction, such as a living will or a health care power of attorney, about care if the patient can't decide (42 CFR 489.100).
- term: Purpose of use
  definition: Why data is used or disclosed, such as treatment, payment or operations, from HL7 v3 PurposeOfUse.
concepts:
- name: Consent to care is not permission to share
  description: Agreeing to a treatment says nothing about who may see the record. In the United States, sharing for treatment, payment and operations needs no consent, while other disclosures need an authorization; in the EU, care data is processed because care needs it (GDPR Article 9(2)(h)), and consent is the basis only for things such as research or marketing.
- name: The answer, not the form
  description: Modules never read consent forms. They ask whether a use or disclosure is allowed, and get permit or deny with the basis and the obligations, such as a notice that must go with the data.
- name: Nothing is deleted
  description: A revoked, expired or mistaken consent changes status and stays, because every past disclosure must be explainable by the consent in force at the time.

businessRules:
- id: BR-H1
  text: Every consent is about one patient whose record is in use, is of a kind listed in consent_kinds, and names its legal basis. Each kind maps to a FHIR consent category.
  rationale: One record per decision, with its basis, is what lets every later disclosure be explained; FHIR R5 Consent models a decision with its category and regulatory basis.
- id: BR-H2
  text: Consent to treatment is never used as permission to disclose, and permission to disclose is never used as consent to treatment. In the European Union, GDPR consent is recorded only for processing whose legal basis is consent, never for care itself.
  rationale: A HIPAA consent for treatment, payment and operations can't stand in for an authorization (45 CFR 164.506(b)(2)); in the EU, care data is processed under GDPR Article 9(2)(h), and calling it consent would let a withdrawal appear to stop care records.
- id: BR-H3
  text: 'In the United States, a HIPAA authorization is active only when it contains every core element: a specific description of the information, who may disclose it, who may receive it, each purpose (at the request of the individual is enough when the patient starts it), an expiration date or event, and the signature and date, with a representative''s authority described. It must also state the right to revoke in writing and how, whether care can be conditioned on signing, and that disclosed information may be redisclosed, all in plain language. An authorization for marketing that a third party pays the organization for says so, and one for a sale of health information says the organization will be paid. A request missing any of them is refused, listing every missing one, and the patient gets a copy when the organization asked for the authorization.'
  rationale: These are the core elements and required statements of a valid authorization (45 CFR 164.508(a)(3)(ii), (a)(4)(ii) and (c)(1) to (4)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H4
  text: In the United States, a disclosure check never relies on an authorization that has expired, whose expiration event is known to have happened, that is incomplete, that is known to be revoked, that breaks the rules on combining or conditioning, or whose material information is known to be false.
  rationale: These are the defects that make an authorization invalid (45 CFR 164.508(b)(2)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H5
  text: In the United States, an authorization for psychotherapy notes can be combined only with another for psychotherapy notes, and no authorization is combined with one that care was conditioned on, except research authorizations combined as the rules allow. Care, payment, enrollment or eligibility is never conditioned on signing an authorization, except research-related treatment, or care given only to create information for a third party.
  rationale: HIPAA limits compound authorizations and prohibits conditioning, with these exceptions (45 CFR 164.508(b)(3) and (4)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H6
  text: In the United States, a Part 2 consent is active only with the patient's name; who may disclose; a specific description of the information; the recipients, which for a single consent for all future treatment, payment and operations can be described generally, and which name an intermediary with its participants by name, or by a general designation limited to those with a treating provider relationship with the patient; each purpose; the right to revoke in writing; an expiration date or event (end of the treatment or none is enough for treatment, payment and operations); the signature, of the minor or of a person authorized for a patient lacking capacity or deceased; the date; for treatment, payment and operations, the statements on redisclosure and on the consequences of refusing; when a recipient is a covered entity or business associate, the statement that it may redisclose as HIPAA allows except in proceedings against the patient; and, if the program will fundraise with the records, the patient's right to opt out of fundraising.
  rationale: These are the required elements of a Part 2 consent; electronic signatures are allowed where law doesn't forbid them, and the rules apply from 16 February 2026 (42 CFR 2.31(a); 89 FR 12472).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H7
  text: In the United States, a use or disclosure of SUD counseling notes needs its own consent, which can be combined only with another consent for SUD counseling notes and on which no care or payment is conditioned.
  rationale: Part 2 protects SUD counseling notes the way HIPAA protects psychotherapy notes (42 CFR 2.31(b)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H8
  text: 'In the United States, a permitted disclosure of Part 2 records comes with the obligation to attach the statement 42 CFR 2.32 requires, and the answer says how the recipient may redisclose: a covered entity or business associate receiving under a consent for treatment, payment and operations may redisclose as HIPAA allows, except in proceedings against the patient.'
  rationale: Every disclosure with consent must carry the Part 2 notice, and redisclosure follows the consent and HIPAA (42 CFR 2.32, 2.33).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H9
  text: In the United States, where state law lets a minor obtain SUD treatment alone, only the minor signs a Part 2 consent; where state law requires a parent's consent to treatment, both the minor and the parent or guardian sign. minor_consent_rules holds the state rules.
  rationale: Part 2 decides who signs for a minor from state law (42 CFR 2.14).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H10
  text: A consent is signed by the patient, or by a representative acting within the scope the Patients module records, whose authority is described on the consent. Signatures are on paper or electronic as signature_methods allows; an electronic signature is accepted unless the law requires another form for that kind.
  rationale: A consent signed by someone without authority is invalid; electronic signatures have legal effect in the United States (15 U.S.C. 7001) and can't be refused in the EU only for being electronic (Regulation (EU) No 910/2014, Article 25).
- id: BR-H11
  text: 'A patient, or a representative within their scope, can revoke a consent at any time. A HIPAA authorization or Part 2 consent is revoked in writing, which includes the portal; GDPR consent and EHDS opt-outs are withdrawn as easily as they were given. Revocation takes effect when received and stops future uses only: what was already done in reliance on it stands. The consent becomes inactive and is kept.'
  rationale: HIPAA and Part 2 allow written revocation except for action already taken in reliance (45 CFR 164.508(b)(5); 42 CFR 2.31(a)(6)); GDPR requires withdrawal to be as easy as giving and not to affect earlier processing (Article 7(3)); EHDS opt-outs are reversible (Articles 10(1) and 71(1)).
- id: BR-H12
  text: A consent with an expiration date becomes inactive at the end of that date in the time zone of the organization holding it, and consent.expired is emitted; one ending on an event becomes inactive when staff record the event.
  rationale: An expired authorization is invalid (45 CFR 164.508(b)(2)(i); 42 CFR 2.31(c)(1)); end dates are inclusive, as FHIR periods are.
- id: BR-H13
  text: In the United States, a patient can ask to restrict uses or disclosures for treatment, payment or operations, or disclosures to family and friends. The request names the services or encounters it covers. A privacy officer agrees or declines, except that a request not to tell a health plan about a service the patient paid for in full, for payment or operations and where no law requires it, must be agreed. An agreed restriction binds every disclosure check, including uses for treatment, payment and operations, except emergency treatment, disclosures required to HHS, the facility directory, and the public-interest disclosures of 45 CFR 164.512.
  rationale: Patients may request restrictions, the self-pay restriction must be agreed, and an agreed restriction doesn't block disclosures to HHS, the directory or the public-interest disclosures (45 CFR 164.522(a)(1)(i) to (vi)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H14
  text: In the United States, an agreed restriction ends when the patient agrees in writing, or orally with that documented, or when the organization tells the patient it is ending it, which then applies only to information created or received after telling them and never to a self-pay restriction.
  rationale: These are the only ways to terminate a restriction (45 CFR 164.522(a)(2)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H15
  text: In the United States, a patient can ask the organization not to share their electronic health information. The request is documented, applied the same way for every patient, and ended only as a restriction is (BR-H14).
  rationale: Honouring such a request isn't information blocking when these conditions are met (45 CFR 171.202(e)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H16
  text: In the United States, when care_access_policy is set, disclosure checks withhold the reproductive health information it covers, labelled SEX, from the access, exchange or use it names, and nothing more. A patient's explicit request to share that information despite the risk, recorded as share_despite_risk, overrides it. The 2024 HIPAA attestation for reproductive health information is not applied, because a court vacated it.
  rationale: Withholding to reduce exposure to legal action isn't information blocking when tailored and based on a written policy, and the patient can nullify it (45 CFR 171.206(a) and (b)); the attestation in 45 CFR 164.509 was vacated nationwide in Purl v. HHS (N.D. Tex. 2025), and the appeal was dismissed on 10 September 2025.
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H17
  text: In the United States, a patient is given the Notice of Privacy Practices by their first service, which starts when the Encounters module reports their first encounter started, or as soon as practicable after emergency treatment, and is asked for a written acknowledgment; if it isn't obtained, the good-faith attempt and the reason are recorded. Patients whose encounter starts in a department the Organization module reports as a Part 2 program are also told at that admission, or when they regain capacity, that federal law protects their SUD records.
  rationale: Providers must give the notice and try to get an acknowledgment (45 CFR 164.520(c)(2)); Part 2 programs must give their notice at admission (42 CFR 2.22(a)).
  jurisdiction: us
- id: BR-H18
  text: In the European Union, a patient gets privacy information when their data is collected from them. When it comes from someone else, such as a relative or emergency staff, they get it within privacy_information_deadline, at most one month, and in any case by the first contact with them or the first disclosure to another recipient, whichever is sooner.
  rationale: GDPR requires information at collection (Article 13) or, for data obtained elsewhere, within a reasonable period and at the latest one month, at first communication, or at first disclosure to another recipient (Article 14(3)).
  jurisdiction: eu
- id: BR-H19
  text: In the European Union, a GDPR consent is recorded with the exact text and version the patient agreed to, who agreed, when and how, is explicit for health data, is presented apart from other matters, and is never a condition of care.
  rationale: The controller must be able to show consent was given, and it must be distinguishable, explicit for special categories and freely given (GDPR Articles 7(1), 7(2), 7(4) and 9(2)(a)).
  jurisdiction: eu
- id: BR-H20
  text: In the European Union, a patient can restrict health professionals' access to all or parts of their data. They are told first that this may affect their care, and ehds_restriction_rules applies the member state's rules and safeguards. Disclosure checks for health professionals then answer that the restricted data is to be left out silently, and modules show nothing in its place, so the restriction itself is never visible to them, except access to protect the patient's vital interests, which is logged for the patient to see.
  rationale: EHDS gives the right to restrict access, requires the patient to be told of its effect, keeps the fact of a restriction hidden from providers, and allows vital-interest access that is logged (EHDS Articles 8 and 11(5)); these apply from 26 March 2029 (Article 105).
  jurisdiction: eu
- id: BR-H21
  text: In the European Union, where primary_use_opt_out says the member state provides it, a patient can opt out of access to their data through access services, and opt back in; vital-interest access follows the member state's rules.
  rationale: Member states may give this right, which must be reversible (EHDS Article 10).
  jurisdiction: eu
- id: BR-H22
  text: In the European Union, a patient can opt out of secondary use at any time without giving a reason, and opt back in. consent.opt_out_changed is emitted so their data is left out of datasets for permits approved after the opt-out; permits approved before it are unaffected, and so is any use member state law allows under Article 71(4).
  rationale: EHDS gives this reversible right, limits its effect to later permits, and lets member states make such data available under strict conditions (EHDS Article 71(1), (3) and (4)); Chapter IV applies from 26 March 2029 (Article 105).
  jurisdiction: eu
- id: BR-H23
  text: A disclosure check names the patient, the data (its sensitivity codes, categories or records), the purpose, the requester and the recipient, and gets permit or deny with its basis (the consents or the law relied on) and obligations, such as a notice to attach or a limit on redisclosure. A deny that applies wins over a permit, except where law requires the disclosure or a rule here names an override, such as emergency use. When a patient records a permit that conflicts with a deny they made themselves, they are asked whether to end the deny. A use the law allows without consent, such as treatment within the organization, is permitted with basis law, unless a narrower rule applies to that data. One check can cover many records and answers for each. Every check and its answer is logged. A module may reuse an answer only until a consent event for that patient arrives.
  rationale: Modules need one consistent answer instead of reading forms; the log shows what each disclosure relied on, for audits and the accounting of disclosures.
- id: BR-H24
  text: 'Restricted data can be used or disclosed in an emergency: under an agreed restriction for emergency treatment, with a request that the receiving provider not redisclose it; for Part 2 records to medical personnel in a bona fide emergency, documented at once with the personnel and their facility, who disclosed, when and the nature of the emergency; and in the EU to protect vital interests, logged for the patient. Each is recorded as an emergency use and reviewed by a privacy officer. While break-the-glass access that the Identity and access module reported is active for a patient, a disclosure check from that user with purpose ETREAT is answered permit for restricted data, and the emergency use is recorded automatically.'
  rationale: HIPAA, Part 2 and the EHDS each allow emergency use under these conditions (45 CFR 164.522(a)(1)(iii) and (iv); 42 CFR 2.51; EHDS Article 11(5)).
- id: BR-H25
  text: Informed consent to a treatment or procedure records the procedure, the practitioner who obtained it, that risks, benefits and alternatives were discussed in a language the patient understands, and the signature where consent_required_procedures requires one. The patient can withdraw it until the procedure starts, or refuse it; a refusal is recorded with the risks of refusing that were explained, and consent.refused or consent.revoked tells the modules planning the procedure. In an emergency without consent, the clinician records why.
  rationale: Patients have the right to make informed decisions, including to refuse treatment (42 CFR 482.13(b)(2)); consent obtained in a language the patient doesn't understand isn't informed, and US providers must offer language assistance (45 CFR 92.201); which procedures need written consent is set by law and by the organization.
- id: BR-H26
  text: Whether an adult patient has executed an advance directive is recorded, with its kind and document, at the moment advance_directive_information_at sets for the organization, after giving written information on their rights. Care is never conditioned on whether they have one, and the orders a directive leads to are the Orders module's.
  rationale: Providers must document in a prominent part of the record whether the patient has an advance directive and must not condition care on it (42 CFR 489.102(a)(2) and (a)(3)); when depends on the kind of provider (489.102(b)).
- id: BR-H27
  text: A patient can opt out of a contact channel for a purpose, such as text reminders, by any reasonable means, including replying stop. The opt-out is honoured without delay and within contact_opt_out_deadline, and the Patients module is answered from it for that channel and purpose only, so an opt-out of fundraising texts doesn't stop appointment reminders.
  rationale: In the United States, consent to calls and texts can be revoked by any reasonable means and must be honoured within ten business days (47 CFR 64.1200(a)(10)); a patient's choice of channel should be respected everywhere.
- id: BR-H28
  text: When the Patients module merges two records, the consents of both move to the remaining record; where they conflict, the deny applies until a privacy officer reviews them. When it undoes a merge, consents recorded since are listed for review. When it records a death, consents stay in force until they end, and an estate representative can revoke them. When a representative's authority ends, consents they signed are listed for review and the patient is asked to confirm or revoke them. When it marks a record entered in error, its consents are listed for review.
  rationale: A consent must follow the right person; a conflict resolved toward deny protects the patient until a person decides; HIPAA protects a deceased person's information for 50 years and treats an executor as their personal representative (45 CFR 164.502(f) and (g)(4)).
- id: BR-H29
  text: Consents, revocations, notices with their acknowledgments, emergency uses and checks are kept for six years from when they were created or last in effect, whichever is later, or for consent_retention if longer, and while a legal hold the Patients module reports applies. Erasure is refused until then.
  rationale: HIPAA requires documentation to be kept six years from creation or last effective date (45 CFR 164.530(j)(2)); GDPR Article 17(3)(b) and (e) exempt records needed for legal obligations and claims.
- id: BR-H30
  text: A consent recorded on the wrong patient or twice is marked entered in error with a reason; it no longer counts in disclosure checks, and it is kept.
  rationale: Deleting it would leave earlier disclosures unexplained; FHIR marks such a consent entered-in-error.
- id: BR-H31
  text: When this module can't be reached, modules allow use for treatment inside the organization of data whose treatment use needs no consent, and deny every other use and disclosure until it answers.
  rationale: Blocking care because a consent service is down harms the patient, while a disclosure outside the organization can't be undone.
- id: BR-H32
  text: Every change sends the revision it read; a stale revision fails.
  rationale: Two staff recording on one consent must not overwrite each other.
- id: BR-H33
  text: In the United States, the law allows less without consent for three kinds of data. Psychotherapy notes are used without an authorization only by their author for treatment, for the organization's supervised training, to defend it in a legal action the patient brings, and for disclosures HIPAA requires or permits for them, such as to HHS, for oversight of the author, to a coroner or to avert a serious threat. workflows:
- name: Sign an authorization
  trigger: A patient asks for records to be sent to someone, or the organization asks for an authorization
  actor: Registrar
  steps:
  - Start a draft with the information, recipient, purpose and expiry
  - The service lists any missing element
  - The patient, or their representative, signs on paper or in the portal
  - The patient gets a copy
  outcome: An active authorization disclosure checks can rely on.
- name: Revoke a consent
  trigger: The patient asks to revoke
  actor: Patient
  steps:
  - The patient revokes in the portal or in writing
  - The consent becomes inactive from that moment
  - consent.revoked is emitted
  outcome: No new use or disclosure relies on it; earlier ones stand.
- name: Decide a restriction request
  trigger: A patient asks to restrict a use or disclosure
  actor: Privacy officer
  steps:
  - Record the request as a draft restriction
  - Agree or decline; a self-pay request to a health plan is always agreed
  - Tell the patient, and Billing for a self-pay restriction
  outcome: Disclosure checks honour every agreed restriction.
- name: Check a disclosure
  trigger: A module is about to use or disclose data
  actor: System
  steps:
  - Send the patient, data labels, purpose, requester and recipient
  - Get permit or deny with basis and obligations
  - Apply the obligations, such as attaching the Part 2 statement
  outcome: Every use and disclosure is backed by a logged answer.
- name: Give the privacy notice
  trigger: A patient's first service, or patient.registered with an informant in the EU
  actor: Registrar
  steps:
  - Give the current notice or privacy information
  - Ask for a written acknowledgment, or record the attempt and the reason
  outcome: Every patient has been given the notice, provably.
- name: Use restricted data in an emergency
  trigger: A patient needs emergency treatment and restricted data is needed
  actor: Clinician
  steps:
  - Use or disclose only what the emergency needs
  - Record the emergency use with the personnel, time and nature of the emergency
  - A privacy officer reviews it
  outcome: Care isn't blocked, and the exception is documented.

apiConventions:
- topic: Authentication
  text: Callers authenticate through the Identity and access module and every operation checks the permission in the access matrix.
- topic: Errors
  text: Errors use problem details (application/problem+json) with a code member, such as CONSENT_INCOMPLETE; a validation error lists every failing field.
  reference: RFC 9457
- topic: Pagination
  text: 'Lists are cursor-based: limit and an optional cursor in, next_cursor out.'
- topic: Concurrency
  text: Every change sends the revision it read; a stale revision fails with CONSENT_REVISION_CONFLICT (BR-H32).
- topic: Retries
  text: Every create accepts an Idempotency-Key header.
  reference: IETF draft-ietf-httpapi-idempotency-key-header
- topic: Dates
  text: Dates are ISO 8601 calendar dates, read as the local date where care is given, never a date converted to UTC.
  reference: ISO 8601
- topic: Times
  text: Instants are RFC 3339 timestamps with an offset, stored in UTC; time zones are IANA names.
  reference: RFC 3339
- topic: Personal data in URLs
  text: Names, birth dates and identifiers are sent in request bodies or query parameters over TLS, never in paths, and are never written to request logs.
- topic: Rate limits
  text: A caller over its limit gets 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header.
  reference: RFC 6585, RFC 9110
- topic: Versioning
  text: Additive changes don't change the version. A breaking change ships as a new major version with Deprecation and Sunset headers.
  reference: RFC 9745, RFC 8594
- topic: FHIR
  text: Consents are exchanged as FHIR R5 Consent resources, with status, decision, provisions, grantor and verification.
  reference: HL7 FHIR R5 Consent

deliveryGuarantees:
- id: DG-H1
  text: Events are published at least once; consumers drop one with a source and id they have seen.
  rationale: A relay can publish twice after a crash.
- id: DG-H2
  text: Events about one consent can arrive out of order; each carries its revision, and consumers ignore a lower one. Notice, emergency use and review events carry their time instead.
  rationale: A revoked consent overtaken by an older activation would let a disclosure through.
- id: DG-H3
  text: Events carry ids, kinds and decisions, never the consent document, its provisions or the data it covers.
  rationale: A Part 2 consent shows that a patient is in SUD treatment; events fan out widely.
- id: DG-H4
  text: Every consent event names the patient, so a module that reuses disclosure answers drops those for that patient when one arrives.
  rationale: A cached permit outliving a revocation would let a disclosure through (BR-H23).

Every time a module shares or shows a patient's data, there is a question
behind it: may it? This module records what patients have agreed to, refused
or restricted, proves each decision was made validly, and answers that
question for every other module.

## Consent to care isn't permission to share

A patient who agrees to a colonoscopy hasn't agreed to their results going to
an employer. The two are separate records, and one never stands in for the
other (BR-H2).

## Modules ask, they never read forms

A module about to disclose data asks whether it may, for this purpose, to this
recipient, and gets permit or deny with the reason and what must go with the
data (BR-H23). One answer in one place means the same decision everywhere, and
a log of what every disclosure relied on.

## Emergencies override restrictions, on the record

A restriction must never stop emergency care. Restricted data can be used in an
emergency, and every such use is recorded and reviewed (BR-H24).

## In the European Union, a restriction leaves no trace

When a patient restricts health professionals' access, the restricted data
simply isn't there for them, with no hint it was hidden (BR-H20).
